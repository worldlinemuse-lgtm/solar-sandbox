          Sources by partEach rating and the maker page or datasheet it comes from.
            Weather and sunA typical year for Honesdale, plus 25 recorded winters. Not a forecast.
            • Hour-by-hour typical-year weather for Honesdale from the national solar radiation database, served through PVWatts: direct and diffuse sun, air temperature, wind and ground reflectance.
            • Sun on the panels is worked out for your tilt and direction each hour, with the Perez sky model PVWatts uses and the loss from light glancing off the glass at low angles.
            • Panel temperature uses the Sandia model for your mount type. Each panel's own power-versus-heat figure comes from its datasheet where published.
            • Soiling, mismatch, connection, light-induced and nameplate losses (PVWatts defaults, about 6.8% together) are always on. Wire loss is worked out from your cables. Shade is the extra slider.
            • Snowstorms and Arctic blasts are game events you trigger in the typical year, and how fast that snow melts off is a rough game rule. The typical year puts no snow on the panels by itself.
            • In the recorded winters, NASA POWER's daily snowfall covers the panels on any day with at least 1 mm of water equivalent, about 1 cm of snow at an assumed 10 to 1 ratio. That trigger is an estimate. The snow then slides off using NREL's published model (Marion and others, 2013, as in pvlib): it slides when the air is warm enough for the sun on the panel, faster on steeper panels. You can switch this off in the build settings.
            • The weather file's wind is very light, about 0.2 m/s on average. Light wind makes the panels run warmer in the model, which trims output slightly. That figure wasn't independently confirmed.
            Accuracy checksAutomated checks against independent tools.
            • Yearly sun on the panels matched pvlib (an open-source solar library) within 0.1% at every tilt tested, using the same weather.
            • PVWatts gives 3 to 7% less on steep tilts by default because it assumes rows of panels shading each other. Rerun with widely spaced rows (ground coverage ratio 0.01, the lowest PVWatts accepts), its yearly sun on the panel matched the sandbox within 0.2% at 40°, 60°, 75° and 90° facing south, with no month more than 1.6% apart.
            • The bifacial rear-side estimate at 40° (16.4% extra) is within a point of pvlib's bifacial model (15.7%).
            • Energy in equals energy out plus losses, minute by minute, for every battery, indoors and in a shed, in January, July and over a full year.
            What's estimated or simplifiedLabeled guesses, so treat results as rough.
            • Inverter efficiency changes with load. Victron publishes only peak efficiency and no-load draw, so the shape of the curve and where it peaks are assumptions.
            • Panels with no published temperature figures (the Renogy 320 W and the flexible 100 W) use the most cautious values from the other datasheets.
            • The bifacial panel's rear gain is an estimate and only counts on an open rack. Renogy publishes no bifaciality number.
            • Charging is capped at half each battery's amp-hours or the maker's max, whichever is lower. Round trip is about 95% for lithium and 81% for AGM. The AGM resting-voltage curve is a guess.
            • Charging tapers from 95% charge for lithium and 80% for AGM; where the taper starts is an estimate. Lithium absorption ends after Battle Born's published 30 minutes per 100 Ah of bank, applied to every lithium battery here (LiTime publishes no time), then it floats. The absorption timer restarts each day or when charge falls 5 points below the taper start, which is also an estimate.
            • AGM capacity at high current and in the cold comes from Renogy's spec-sheet tables (for example 54 Ah at the 1-hour rate, 85% at 32°F). The sim drains charge faster to match and multiplies the two effects together, which is an estimate. The tables were read off an image of the sheet by text recognition; the 30-minute figure was re-read character by character as 86.32 A.
            • Battery health scales usable capacity only, not internal resistance. The aging estimate turns each maker's published cycle life into capacity left after 1, 5 and 10 years. The 80% end-of-life point and the calendar loss (1% a year for lithium, 4% for AGM) are estimates, heat and cold don't change them, and a bank that has lost capacity is assumed to keep cycling at the same rate.
            • The self-heating battery's warm-up speed comes in three settings, fast, middle and slow. Each warming rate fits both of LiTime's ranges: 70 to 90 minutes from 14°F and 100 to 150 minutes from −4°F. The sim steps minute by minute, which adds about a minute, so from 14°F the slow setting takes 91 minutes. The heater needs at least 10 A from the charger (LiTime manual).
            • In a shed, battery temperature follows the outdoor air with about a 4-hour lag, and the inverter's heat derating uses the outdoor air directly. Indoors both sit at an assumed 64°F.
            • Chargers run at their full watts until the battery is full, then stop. Real chargers slow down near full, but the energy is about the same.
            • Appliances run exactly on the clock you set. Several of the same appliance start together, the worst case for surge. Thermostat appliances run up to 60% of the time in deep cold, a rough guess.
            • Wire heating with current is estimated. Ampacity uses NEC Table 310.16 and the temperature corrections of 310.15(B)(1), both checked against independent reproductions. The bundling factors of 310.15(C)(1) and the 690.8 and 110.14(C) rules (PV current ×1.25 twice, terminal temperature limits) were checked only against search summaries, not the code text itself. A check stays "Not verified" until you enter the air temperature, bundling and ratings.
            • Short-circuit current is never verified: no battery maker here publishes one. Battle Born gives 7 milliohms internal resistance; LiTime's 12 V manuals give at most 40 milliohms, so a fault current worked out from it is a minimum. Fuse interrupt ratings are Blue Sea Systems' own: Class T (5114) 20,000 A at 125 V DC, ANL (5127) 6,000 A at 80 V, MRBF (5183) 10,000 A at 14 V, 5,000 A at 32 V and 2,000 A at 58 V. MEGA fuses are left out because no Littelfuse datasheet was found. No fuse is ever marked as right for your bank.
            • The inverter overload timing is approximate: Victron publishes the continuous, 1-hour and peak points, and the sim's behavior between them is a simple rule.
            • The recorded winters are NASA POWER hourly data for Honesdale, Nov 1 to Feb 28, 2001–02 through 2025–26, with Feb 29 dropped. It's satellite and weather-model data on a coarse grid, not a ground station. Its wind averages about 2.7 m/s over Nov–Feb, against 0.2 m/s in the typical-year file. Measured on the starting panels, that extra wind makes them run cooler and produce about 1.1% more over the 25 winters than the same weather with 0.2 m/s wind (0.9% to 1.3% by winter). Months outside Nov–Feb stay typical-year weather.
            • The synthetic worst week is made-up weather: seven of the dullest December days from the typical-year file strung together. The worst recorded stretch and the recorded winters are real sequences of days. The failure rate is a count over 25 past winters: each recorded winter is a historical-weather scenario, not a prediction, so the rate is not the probability that your system fails next winter. With only 25 winters its range is wide.
            • Prices are single-unit or pack prices on the day checked, before tax and shipping. Each line in the cost list says whether it's confirmed (with the date), your own purchase price, an estimate, or missing. Prices change often.
            Not modeled: you still need theseThe sim does none of this. It says nothing about whether an installation is safe or meets code.
            • Transfer switch or generator interlock. Feeding house circuits from an inverter or generator needs a listed transfer switch or panel interlock so power can't flow back onto the grid lines. Not modeled. Follow the inverter's and the switch maker's manuals and use a licensed electrician.
            • Household wiring. Breakers, subpanels, branch circuits, receptacles and which circuits go on which leg aren't modeled. The 120/240 V leg split here is only a load estimate.
            • Grounding and bonding. Equipment grounding, ground rods, the neutral-ground bond in inverters and generators, and array frame grounding aren't modeled. Victron's MultiPlus-II manual covers its ground relay; the rest needs an electrician.
            • System integration. Settings and communication between the inverter, controllers, battery monitor and battery (charge voltages, BMS signals, GX device setup), and the rules for two units in split phase, aren't modeled. Follow Victron's manuals and the battery maker's settings.
            • Permits, inspection, racking structure and wind or snow loads on the mount. Not modeled.
            • Wire and fuse checks here are table math against the code tables listed in Sources. They are not an approval: have an electrician size and check the real installation.
